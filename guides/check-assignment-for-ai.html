How to Check Your Assignment for AI Writing Before You Submit It

Published 23 Nov 2026 · Updated 23 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · Study

Teachers run student work through AI checkers. Check your own assignment first, read the score honestly, and fix the patterns that make text look machine-made.

Result

More and more teachers run student work through AI checkers before they read it. A high "AI-written" score can mean extra questions, a lower mark, or a request to rewrite — even when you wrote every word yourself. The problem is that checkers do not detect AI. They guess, from patterns: the same sentence length again and again, the same stock phrases, perfect grammar with no personal detail. If your writing has those patterns, it can look machine-made whether a machine wrote it or not.

This guide shows you how to check your own assignment before you submit it, read the score honestly, and fix the patterns that make text look AI-written. It takes five minutes. It will not turn AI-written work into your own — nothing can, and no honest guide would promise that. It makes your own writing read like a person wrote it.

What you need

  • The assignment text, finished or nearly finished.
  • About five minutes, and a willingness to rewrite a few sentences.
  • No account and nothing to install — the checker runs free in your browser.

Check the whole assignment, not just one paragraph. Short samples are hard to judge — a checker can only work with what you give it, and thirty words tell it almost nothing.

1 Paste your text into the free checker

Open the AI Checker on the Student Assignment Helper site. Paste your text — at least 30 characters, up to 30,000 — and run the check. No upload, no account. The tool is a local heuristic: it looks for the patterns AI writing usually has, and gives you a score with the reasons behind it.

2 Read the score and the reasons, not just the number

You get a score and a verdict — "Likely human-written", "Mixed — some AI-like patterns", "Likely AI-written" or "Very likely AI-written". The number alone tells you little. Read the reasons: they name the exact phrases the checker flagged — words like "moreover", "furthermore", "in conclusion", "it is important to note", "crucial", "pivotal", "utilize" — and whether your sentences are all the same length. Those reasons are your to-do list.

When we tested the checker, a paragraph packed with those stock phrases scored 36, "Mixed", with seven flagged phrases listed by name. The same length of plain, personal writing — short sentences, a place name, a detail only the writer would know — scored 12, "Likely human-written". The difference was not the topic. It was the patterns.

3 Cut the stock phrases

Delete the phrases the checker flagged, and do not replace them with other stock phrases. "Moreover" can usually be deleted entirely — the next sentence makes the point on its own. "In conclusion" can go; the last paragraph is obviously the conclusion. "It is important to note that" can be cut to nothing, and the sentence underneath gets stronger. "Utilize" is just "use". "Crucial" and "pivotal" are words people almost never say out loud — if you would not say it to a friend, do not write it in an assignment.

4 Vary your sentence lengths

AI writing loves medium sentences, one after another, all about the same length. Real writing mixes them. Break one long sentence into two short ones. Then let one sentence run a little longer, because it carries a full thought. A very short sentence — three to seven words — wakes the reader up. Read the paragraph aloud: if every sentence lands with the same rhythm, rewrite two of them.

5 Add one detail only you would know

The strongest sign of human writing is a specific detail. A place, a number, a name, a moment — "our school had one computer room with ten old PCs" beats "the institution possessed limited technological resources" every time. No story needed on every line. One real detail per paragraph is enough to break the pattern, and it makes the writing better in its own right: markers remember specific work.

6 Check again, then stop

Paste the revised text and check once more. If the score fell and the flagged phrases are gone, submit it. Do not chase a perfect score — rewriting until the number hits zero usually makes the writing worse, not better. One check, one fix, one re-check is enough.

What this cannot do

No checker can prove who wrote a text, and no score is a verdict. A low score does not prove you wrote it, and a high score does not prove you did not. Teachers know this, and the honest ones treat a score as a reason to talk, not as proof. This guide cannot make AI-written work yours, either. If a machine wrote your assignment, the honest fix is to write it yourself — the checker only helps you polish writing that is already yours.

The checker also struggles with short texts and with technical writing, where standard phrases are normal. A lab report full of standard terms will always look more "AI-like" than a personal essay. Judge the reasons, not just the number.

Tips for writing that reads human

  • Write the first draft in your own words, fast. Do not polish as you go. Polishing too early is what produces the stiff, stock-phrase style checkers flag.
  • Use contractions. "Don't", "can't" and "it's" read like a person. Formal writing without a single contraction reads like a manual.
  • Keep every fact the same. When you rewrite a sentence, keep the numbers, names and dates exactly as they were. Changing facts to sound natural is worse than sounding stiff.
  • Read it aloud. If a sentence sounds strange spoken, it reads strange too. Your ear catches robotic phrasing faster than your eye.
  • Keep your own voice in the final draft. The goal is not to sound like nobody. It is to sound like you.

Frequently asked questions

Will a low score guarantee my teacher accepts my work?

No. A score is an estimate from one tool, and your teacher may use a different one — or none at all. A low score helps, but clear, specific, well-argued writing is what actually earns the mark.

Can I just run AI-written text through a humanizer and submit it?

You can, but you should not. Submitting work you did not write as your own is plagiarism at most schools, and a humanizer does not change that. Use the humanizer on your own drafts, to fix stiff phrasing — not to disguise work that is not yours.

Why does my own writing score high sometimes?

Because formal school writing and AI writing share the same habits: stock phrases, even sentence lengths, no personal detail. That is exactly what this guide fixes — and it is why the reasons matter more than the number.
