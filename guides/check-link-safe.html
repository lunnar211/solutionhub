How to Check If a Link Is Safe Before You Click It

A 30-second habit that stops phishing, fake logins, and malware. Hover, read the address, and check the site before you tap.

Most online scams start with one click. A message says your account will be closed, a delivery is waiting, or you have won something — and the link inside goes to a fake page designed to steal your password or install malware. The page looks real. The message sounds urgent. The only defence that always works is a 30-second check before you click. This guide teaches you that habit.

What you need

  • Thirty seconds and a suspicious mind.
  • Nothing to install. Your browser already has the tools.

The core idea is simple: a link is not what its text says — it is where it actually goes. "Click here to verify your bank account" can point anywhere. Your job is to see the real destination before your finger or mouse commits you to it.

Step 1: Stop and ask why you got this message

Did you expect it? Did you order a package, or does the message claim a delivery you never ordered? Is it urgent — "act now or your account will be closed"? Urgency is the scammer's favourite tool. Real companies rarely threaten to close your account in the next hour. If the message creates panic, that is your first warning sign. Slow down.

Step 2: Hover over the link — do not click it

On a computer, move your mouse over the link and look at the bottom-left corner of the browser: it shows the real address. On a phone, press and hold the link (do not tap it) to preview where it goes. Read the address carefully, character by character. Scammers rely on you skimming. Look for these tricks:

  • Misspelled brands: "goggle.com", "arnazon.com", "paypa1.com" — close, but not the real site.
  • Extra words: "google.security-check.net" is not Google. The real domain is the part just before the first single slash — everything before that is the address, and subdomains do not count.
  • Shortened links: bit.ly and similar links hide the destination entirely. Do not trust them from strangers.
  • HTTP instead of HTTPS: a login page without the padlock is not safe, full stop.

Step 3: Check the sender the same way

Read the sender's full email address, not just the display name. "Google Support" as a display name means nothing — the real address might be "support@google-security-alerts.xyz". A real company emails from its own domain. If the address is a free mailbox or a strange domain, the message is almost certainly fake.

Step 4: Go to the site yourself

If the message claims to be from your bank, your email provider, or a delivery company, do not use the link. Open your browser and type the address yourself, or open the company's own app. Then check your account there. If there really is a problem, it will be visible on the real site. If there is nothing there, the message was fake.

Step 5: Check the link with a scanner when in doubt

If a link looks suspicious but you are not sure, paste it into a free URL scanner such as VirusTotal or Google Safe Browsing's transparency report. These check the address against databases of known bad sites. A clean result does not prove a new scam site is safe — very new scam pages are not in the databases yet — but a bad result proves it is dangerous. When in doubt, delete the message.

What to do if you already clicked

Do not panic, but act fast. If you entered a password, change it immediately from the real site, and change it everywhere else you used the same password. Turn on two-factor authentication. If you entered card details, call your bank and ask them to block the card. If you downloaded something, delete it and run a scan. And tell the people who might get a copy of the same scam — a quick warning helps others more than you think.

Tips that keep you safe

  • Never log in through an emailed link. Always type the address yourself or use the app.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication on your email and bank accounts. It stops most account theft even if a password leaks.
  • Use different passwords for important accounts. A password manager makes this easy.
  • Be extra careful with attachments. Unexpected files, especially .exe, .apk, or Office files with "enable macros", are a classic trap.
  • Teach the habit to family. Scammers target older relatives and children first, because they are the least suspicious.

Frequently asked questions

Can a link be dangerous even if I only visit the page?

Usually just visiting a page is not enough to infect a modern, updated browser — the danger starts when you enter information or download something. But do not rely on that: if a page looks wrong, close it.

What if the link is from someone I know?

Accounts get hacked and send scam links to all their contacts. If a friend sends a strange link, ask them in person or on a different channel before clicking.

Does the padlock mean a site is safe?

No. The padlock only means the connection is encrypted — scammers use HTTPS too. A padlock does not prove the site is honest.

What is the one rule to remember?

Never log in or pay through a link in a message. Go to the real site yourself. That one habit stops almost every phishing attack.
