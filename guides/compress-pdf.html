How to Compress a PDF Without Losing Quality

Shrink a PDF that is too big to upload or email, without making the text blurry. Free, in your browser.

You have finished the document. Then the upload form says: "File too large. Maximum 5 MB." Your PDF is 18 MB. This happens to almost everyone, and the fix takes about a minute — if you compress the file the right way. Done wrong, compression turns crisp text into a blurry mess. Done right, nobody can tell the difference.

This guide explains what makes PDFs large, how to shrink one without losing readable quality, and how to check the result before you send it.

What you need

  • The PDF that is too large.
  • A modern browser.
  • Two minutes, and a backup copy of the original file.

Start by understanding why your file is big. In most cases the answer is images: scanned pages and photos inside the PDF take up far more space than text. A ten-page document of pure text is usually under 1 MB. The same ten pages scanned as images can be 20 MB. Compression works by making those images smaller and leaner — the text itself is barely touched.

Step 2: Upload the large PDF

Add the file and let the tool read it. Note the starting size — you will want to compare it with the result. If the file is a scan, expect a big drop; if it is mostly text, expect a small one. That is normal, not a sign that something failed.

Step 3: Choose a sensible level

Compression is a trade-off between size and image quality. For documents that will be read on screen or printed normally, a medium setting is the right choice: images get smaller, text stays sharp. Do not pick the most aggressive setting for a document with photos or certificates — it can make stamps and signatures hard to read. If the tool offers one standard pass, use it first and check the result before trying anything stronger.

Step 4: Download and check the result

Download the compressed file and open it. Zoom to 100% and read a page. Then zoom to 150% and look at any photo, stamp, or signature. If the text is crisp and the images are clear, you are done. If the text looks soft, go back and compress the original again at a gentler setting. Never compress an already-compressed file a second time — each pass throws away more detail, and the damage adds up.

How small is small enough?

Most upload forms accept 5 MB or 10 MB. Email attachments usually work up to 20–25 MB. A good target is under 5 MB for forms and under 10 MB for email. If your file is still over the limit after one careful pass, the problem is usually one huge image — check whether a single scanned page is responsible, and consider whether that page could be a smaller scan.

Tips for a clean result

  • Always compress a copy of the original. Keep the big file safe until the small one is accepted.
  • Compress once, gently. One medium pass beats two harsh passes every time.
  • Check the pages that matter most — the photo page, the signature page, any page with a stamp. Those suffer first.
  • Do not compress a file that is already small. Below 1 MB, there is nothing meaningful left to save.
  • Rename the result clearly, for example "application-small.pdf", so you never send the wrong one by mistake.

Frequently asked questions

Will compression make my text blurry?

Not if you use a sensible setting. Text is barely affected; images carry almost all of the savings. Check the result at 100% zoom before sending.

How much smaller will my PDF get?

It depends on the content. Scanned documents often shrink by 60–80%. Text-only documents shrink very little, because there was little to remove.

Can I compress a PDF twice to make it even smaller?

You can, but you should not. Each pass removes more detail. If one pass did not reach your target size, the file probably has one very large image that needs a different fix.

Is my file uploaded to a server?

No. Compression runs in your browser on your own device.
