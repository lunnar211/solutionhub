How to Find Real Business Leads for Free

Find real businesses that could become customers — with names, phones, and websites — from public data. Free 30 leads a day, no scraping, no invented emails.

Finding customers starts with finding businesses. Not names bought from a shady list, not emails guessed from patterns — real businesses, with real addresses, phones, and websites, that you can actually contact. The good news is that this information is public. The bad news is that collecting it by hand takes forever. A lead tool does the collecting; your job is the contacting.

This guide shows you how to find real business leads for free, what a good lead looks like, and the honest limits of every free method — including the ones that waste your time.

What you need

  • A clear idea of who your customer is — what kind of business, and where.
  • A way to contact them — an email address or a phone, found honestly.
  • Thirty minutes to search, and an hour a week to follow up.

Start with the customer, not the tool. "Dentists in New York" is a search you can act on. "Businesses" is not. The narrower your search, the better your leads — and the better your replies. Pick one type of business and one place, and search that properly before you move on.

The tool searches real public business data and returns businesses with names, categories, locations, phones, and websites. Step 2: Check each lead before you contact it

A lead is only good if the business is real, open, and relevant. Open the business's own website and check three things: does it exist and look active, does it match what you sell, and is there a real published email address or phone number? If there is no published email, do not guess one — guessed emails bounce, annoy people, and damage your sender reputation. A lead without a real contact is not a lead; it is a name on a list. Skip it and move on.

Step 3: Contact one at a time, like a person

Write a short, personal message to each business. Say who you are, what you noticed about their business, and what you offer — in three or four sentences. Ask for one small thing: a 15-minute call. Do not send the same message to a hundred businesses at once. Bulk blasts get ignored, marked as spam, and sometimes get your email account blocked. One honest message to ten right businesses beats a hundred identical messages to anyone.

Step 4: Follow up, then follow up again

Most replies come after the second or third message, not the first. People are busy; your first email was probably seen and forgotten. Wait three or four days, then send a short follow-up: one line reminding them what you offered, and the same small ask. If they reply "not interested" or ask you to stop, stop immediately and remove them from your list. Respect is not just polite — it is what keeps your email working.

What a good lead looks like

  • Real and reachable: a working website or phone number you verified yourself.
  • Relevant: the business can actually use what you sell.
  • Right size: a small business is more likely to answer than a giant company with a gatekeeper.
  • Contactable: a published email address on their own website — never an invented one.

Tips for better results

  • Search narrow, then widen. One town and one business type first. Expand only when the narrow search runs dry.
  • Keep a simple list. Business name, contact, date you wrote, reply or no reply. A spreadsheet is enough.
  • Track your replies. If one type of business answers and another never does, spend your time where the answers are.
  • Never buy lead lists. Bought lists are old, wrong, and often illegal to mail to. Public data, checked by you, is better.
  • Expect a low reply rate. Even good outreach gets replies from a small percentage. That is normal, not failure.
