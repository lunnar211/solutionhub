How to Write a Follow-Up Email That Gets Replies

Most replies come after the second or third email, not the first. A short, honest follow-up structure that respects the reader and gets answers.

You sent a good email. No reply. You wait a day, then a week, and start to wonder if you did something wrong. Probably not — most people are busy, and most replies come after the second or third message, not the first. The follow-up is where most deals, jobs, and answers are actually won. But most follow-ups are terrible: too long, too pushy, or so vague they say nothing. This guide shows you how to write one that gets replies.

What you need

  • The original email you sent, and the date you sent it.
  • One clear thing you want: a call, an answer, or a yes/no.
  • Five minutes, and the discipline to keep it short.

Before you write anything, check one thing: did you actually ask for something? Many first emails describe an offer and end without a question. No question, no reply. A follow-up is your chance to fix that — ask one specific question, and make it easy to answer.

Step 1: Wait the right amount of time

Wait three to four business days after your first email. Less than that looks impatient. More than a week, and they have forgotten you entirely. If you are following up a second time, wait five to seven days. Three touches in total is a good rule: the first email, then two follow-ups. After that, stop — more messages become pestering, not persistence.

Step 2: Write a subject line that helps, not tricks

Do not write "Re: Re: Re: URGENT" or pretend you have spoken before when you have not. Use the same thread as your first email, so the reader sees the history, and add a short honest line: "Quick follow-up — free demo?" or "Following up on Tuesday's email". The reader should know in one second that this is a polite nudge, not a new sales pitch.

Step 3: Say why you are writing in one sentence

Open with the point. "Following up on my email from Tuesday about your front desk." That is enough. Do not re-paste your whole first email, do not apologise three times, and do not explain your life story. The reader is busy; respect their time and they will respect your message.

Step 4: Repeat the offer in one line, then ask one question

One line on what you offer, then one question. For example: "I help dental clinics answer every call with an AI receptionist, so no patient is missed. Would a free 15-minute demo call be useful?" That is the whole email. One offer, one question, one easy answer. If they can reply "yes, Tuesday works" without thinking, you have written it right.

Step 5: Give them a graceful way out

End with a line that lets them say no easily: "If now is not the right time, just let me know." This sounds soft, but it works — people reply more often when saying no is easy, because it feels safe to answer at all. And when someone says "not now", you have a real answer instead of silence. Always include an opt-out: "Reply STOP and I won't email again." Honour it immediately when anyone uses it.

What not to do

  • Do not guilt-trip. "I have emailed three times and you have not replied" makes you look desperate, not professional.
  • Do not fake a connection. "Great chatting yesterday" when you never spoke is a lie, and people can tell.
  • Do not add new offers every time. Changing your offer in each follow-up makes you look unreliable.
  • Do not follow up the same day. It reads as pressure, not enthusiasm.

Tips that actually help replies

  • Send on Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday morning. Monday inboxes are full; Friday emails get buried over the weekend.
  • Keep it under 80 words. Short emails get read. Long ones get saved for later — which means never.
  • Use their name and their business. One specific detail proves this is not a mass email.
  • Track what you sent. A simple list — who, when, what you asked, reply or no reply — stops you from following up twice by mistake.

Frequently asked questions

How many follow-ups should I send?

Two follow-ups after the first email is a good rule — three touches in total. After that, stop and move on. Persistence is good; pestering is not.

What if they never reply at all?

Move on. Silence is an answer, even if it is not the one you wanted. Spend your next email on someone new instead of your fifth message to someone who is not interested.

Should I follow up by phone instead?

Only if you have a real reason to call and the business publishes a phone number. A call out of nowhere to a business that never asked for one is usually unwelcome. Email first.

Is it rude to follow up?

No — a short, polite follow-up is normal business. It becomes rude only when it is frequent, pushy, or ignores a "no".
