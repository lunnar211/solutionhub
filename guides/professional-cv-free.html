How to Make a Professional CV for Free

Build a clean, honest CV that gets read — free, with no template trap. What to include, what to leave out, and how to tailor it for each job.

Your CV has one job: get you an interview. It has about six seconds to do it — that is how long a recruiter spends on a first scan. A good CV is not the longest one or the prettiest one. It is the one where the right information is easy to find, everything is true, and nothing wastes the reader's time. You can make one for free, and this guide shows you how.

What you need

  • Your real work history, education, and skills — written down honestly.
  • A plain document editor — Google Docs, Microsoft Word, or LibreOffice.
  • One hour, and a friend willing to read the result.

Before you open a template, write the raw facts on paper: every job with dates, every qualification, and three things you actually did in each role — not your job title, what you did. "Handled customer emails and resolved complaints" is useful. "Responsible for customer service" says nothing. The CV is built from facts, not adjectives.

Step 1: Choose a clean, simple layout

Use a plain one-column layout with clear headings and plenty of white space. Your name at the top, then contact details, then a two-line summary, then experience, then education, then skills. One page if you have less than ten years of experience; two pages at most if you have more. Avoid coloured borders, fancy fonts, tables, and graphics — they break in the software employers use to read CVs, and they distract from your words.

Step 2: Write the summary in two lines

The summary is not your life story. It is two lines that say what you do and what you are looking for. For example: "IT support professional with two years of experience in documentation and customer support. Looking for a remote support role." That is enough. A recruiter who wants detail will read the rest; a recruiter who does not will still know who you are.

Step 3: List experience newest first, with results

For each job, write the job title, the employer, the dates, and three bullet points about what you actually did and what changed because of it. Use numbers where you can: "answered 40 support tickets a day", "cut response time from a day to two hours", "trained two new staff". Numbers are believable; adjectives are not. If you have a gap in employment, do not hide it and do not invent a job — a short honest line is always better than a lie that falls apart in an interview.

Step 4: Education, skills, and nothing else that does not help

List your education with the institution, the qualification, and the years. Then list skills you can actually do — "MS Office 365, Google Workspace, documentation systems" — not skills you once watched a video about. Leave out your date of birth, your photo (unless the employer asks for it), your marital status, and your hobbies, unless a hobby is directly relevant to the job. None of these help you get hired, and some can hurt you.

Step 5: Tailor it for every application

One CV for every job is a mistake. Read the job description and move the most relevant experience to the top. If the job wants documentation skills, put your documentation work first. If it wants customer support, lead with that. This takes ten minutes per application and doubles your chances. Keep one master CV with everything, and make a short tailored copy for each job.

Step 6: Proofread, then get someone else to proofread

Read the whole CV aloud. Then ask a friend to read it. Then save it as a PDF — always PDF, never a Word file, unless the employer specifically asks for Word — and name it clearly: "Dipesh-Karki-CV.pdf", not "CV-final-v3.pdf". A typo in your own name or a wrong phone number is the most common, most avoidable reason a good CV gets rejected.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay for a CV template or a CV writer?

No. A clean free layout and honest words beat an expensive design every time. Pay for help only if you need a professional review, not a template.

How long should my CV be?

One page if you have under ten years of experience. Two pages at most after that. Nobody reads page three.

Should I include a photo?

Only if the employer asks for one, or if it is normal in that country or industry. In many places a photo adds nothing and can invite bias.

What if I have no work experience?

Lead with education, projects, internships, and anything you have done that shows reliability — volunteering, coursework, personal projects. A short honest CV beats a padded one.
