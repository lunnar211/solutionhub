How to Remove Background Noise From Audio for Free

Clean up a voice recording — remove hiss, hum, and room noise — free in your browser. Trim, normalise, and download a clean WAV.

You recorded a voice note, a class, or a podcast episode — and behind your voice there is a fan, traffic, a humming fridge, or the echo of an empty room. The recording is usable, but it sounds unprofessional, and every listener notices the noise before they notice what you said. You do not need a studio or paid software to fix the worst of it. A free browser tool can clean it up in minutes.

This guide shows you how to remove background noise from audio for free, using a browser-only cleaner. It works on voice recordings, interviews, lessons, and podcasts. It will not make a bad microphone sound like a studio one, but it will make a decent recording sound clean.

What you need

  • The audio file — MP3, WAV, or a recording from your phone.
  • A modern browser.
  • Headphones, so you can actually hear the difference.
  • A backup copy of the original recording.

Before you start, listen to the whole recording once with headphones and note the problems: constant hiss, a low hum, sudden bumps, long silences, or parts where the volume jumps up and down. Different problems need different fixes, and knowing what you are hearing keeps you from over-processing the file.

Step 1: Open the free audio cleaner

Open AI Studio's audio cleaner in your browser. It is free, and everything runs on your device — your audio is not uploaded to a server. You will need to be signed in for the tool, and files up to 10 MB are accepted.

Step 2: Upload your audio

Upload the file and let it load. The tool shows you the waveform — the picture of your sound. Loud parts are tall, quiet parts are short, and silence is flat. This picture is your map for the next steps.

Step 3: Gate the noise

The cleaner uses a noise gate, which is a simple idea: sound below a certain loudness is treated as background and turned down, while your voice — which is louder — passes through untouched. Set the gate just above the level of the background hiss. Too low, and the hiss survives. Too high, and the quiet ends of your words get chopped off. Listen after each change and stop as soon as the background is quiet between your sentences.

Step 4: Trim the dead air

Cut the silence at the start and end, and any long pauses in the middle that add nothing. A tight recording sounds professional; a loose one sounds like a mistake. Do not cut so aggressively that the speaker sounds rushed — leave a short natural breath between sentences.

Step 5: Normalise the volume

Normalising sets the loudest part of your recording to a standard level (about -1 dB peak), so the whole file plays at a consistent, comfortable volume. This fixes the classic problem of a recording that is too quiet on one phone and too loud on another. It does not fix distortion — if the original was recorded too loud and already crackles, that damage is permanent.

Step 6: Preview and download

Listen to the cleaned version from start to finish with headphones, comparing it with the original. The background should be quiet, the voice clear, and the volume even. Then download the result as a WAV file — a clean, uncompressed copy you can use anywhere.

What this cannot fix

Be honest about the limits. A noise gate removes constant background noise, not sudden sounds — a dog barking or a door slamming will still be there. It cannot remove another voice talking over yours. It cannot rescue a recording made in a very echoey room, and it cannot fix distortion from a microphone that was too close or too loud. The best fix for all of these is the next recording: a quieter room, the microphone closer to the speaker, and a quick test recording before the real one.

Tips for a clean result

  • Use headphones for every check. Phone speakers hide noise that listeners on headphones will hear.
  • Change one thing at a time. Gate first, listen, then trim, then normalise. If you change everything at once, you will not know what helped and what hurt.
  • Keep the original. Save the cleaned file under a new name and keep the raw recording. You may want to redo it later.
  • Record better next time. A blanket over a table, a closed window, and the phone a hand's width from your mouth beat any amount of cleaning.
